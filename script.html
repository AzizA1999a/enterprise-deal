1Title0:30

Our question today: how does a start-up sell to a giant?

A start-up has a product nobody has heard of. A big company has a supplier it already trusts. We could explain why that is hard. Instead, you are going to feel it.

2You are going to live it1:00

In a minute you will scan a code. Your phone gives you a private role. Most of you run a start-up. The rest are buyers at large companies.

You have ten minutes, on your feet. Sellers: find buyers in your category and pitch them, out loud. When a buyer is interested, ask for their desk number, then write the term sheet on your phone.

Buyers: you can sign one deal. Or you can stay with the supplier you already have. That is always safe.

One warning for buyers. If you sign a start-up and it fails to deliver, you lose all your points.

Slides 6 to 9 · 1 · Why is the door closed?

6The buyer is buying a risk2:00 · 2 clicks

Start on the buyer's side. Buyers: when a start-up put an offer in front of you, what were you afraid of? (take one answer)

Exactly. The buyer is not buying a product. The buyer is buying a risk.

If the start-up fails, the buyer answers for it. If the old supplier fails, nobody blames the buyer.

A study of three thousand B2B buyers found that personal value, what this purchase means for me, has twice the impact of business value. One fear those buyers named: losing their job over a failed purchase.

The pointThe obstacle is the buyer's personal risk, not the product.
7The safest choice is the one you already made2:30

So what does a careful person do? Nothing. In our room, [read: % who stayed] of buyers stayed with what they had.

In the real world, Harvard Business Review reports that 40 to 60 percent of deals are lost to nobody. The customer says they want to buy, then does nothing.

Forrester finds that 86 percent of B2B purchases stall at some point.

And this is old psychology: people stick with the option they already have. It is called status quo bias.

So a start-up's main competitor is not another start-up. It is "do nothing".

If few buyers stayed"Our room was braver than the real world. Remember, nobody's actual job was on the line here."
8A real start-up pitches a committee2:00

And our game was the easy version. You pitched one buyer.

Gartner says a real buying group has five to sixteen people, across as many as four functions. Each of them can say no.

Buyers also spend only 17 percent of their buying time meeting suppliers. That is all suppliers together. A start-up gets a small slice of a small slice.

The pointMany people must agree, and they give you very little time.

Slides 10 to 12 · 2 · What opens the door?

10Four ways in1:30 · 4 clicks

Four ways in.

One, borrow trust: arrive through someone the buyer already trusts.

Two, show proof: customers, certifications, results that others can check.

Three, shrink the risk: a pilot turns one big yes into a small, safe one.

Four, walk in with a partner who is already inside.

Let us test each one against what you just did.

111 · Borrow trust2:30

Some sellers had a card that said: you know this buyer. Hands up if you did.

That connection gave you no points. It gave you information. You could see the buyer's hidden priority and their boss's rule. And the buyer could see how reliable you really were.

Here is what it did: [read: no connection, then the best relationship bar].

In the real world, Bain found that about 90 percent of B2B buyers end up buying from a vendor that was already on their list on day one. If they have never heard of you, you are fighting for what is left.

One honest note. You will see claims online like "84 percent of deals start with a referral". We traced that one. It leads back to a vendor's marketing page. We found no solid public study that compares warm introductions with cold ones. So this room is a small test of exactly that.

If connections did not help in the roomAsk a connected seller: "Did you use what you knew about the buyer?" Often they did not. Knowing someone only helps if you act on what it tells you.
122 · Show proof2:00

Second way: proof. Some of you had a reference customer or a security certificate. Some had nothing at all.

Look at the win rates: [read: the best proof bar, then "No proof shown"].

In the real world, G2 found that 80 percent of organisations require a security or privacy assessment before they buy software. Vanta found that 57 percent of start-ups were asked by prospects to prove their security.

And Geoffrey Moore's point in Crossing the Chasm: mainstream buyers want references from buyers like themselves.

Proof is not for the buyer. It is what the buyer shows their boss.

Slides 13 to 16 · 2 · What opens the door? (continued)

133 · Shrink the risk2:00

Third way: make the first step small. Term sheets with a pilot were signed [read: pilot %] of the time, against [read: no pilot %] without.

A pilot lets the buyer say a small yes. In our game, a buyer with a pilot kept some points even if the start-up failed.

But a warning from the real world. In a survey of corporate executives, 81 percent said fewer than a quarter of their start-up pilots became commercial deals. A pilot gets you in the door, not through it.

Palantir told its investors it wins large accounts with short pilots at no or low cost to the customer, and runs them at a loss.

And McKinsey's advice: a pilot has to be big enough to matter.

The pointA pilot lowers the buyer's risk, but plan from day one how it becomes a contract.
144 · Walk in with a partner2:00

Fourth way. Our game did not have this one. Often the easiest way in is beside someone who is already an approved supplier.

McKinsey found that start-ups that partnered with one business unit were satisfied 84 percent of the time. For company-wide programmes it was 57 percent. So look for one unit with a real problem, not the innovation department.

And ask who already sells to your buyer. Microsoft says 95 percent of its commercial revenue flows through its partners.

15And price? It was not the way in2:00

What about price? Many sellers went straight to their floor. In our room, [read: % not the cheapest] of signed deals were not the cheapest offer that buyer had.

Bain asked buyers what matters most. They said cost. Then Bain measured what actually drives their loyalty. Cost reduction came 27th out of 36.

Only 14 percent of buyers see enough difference between suppliers to pay more. When you look the same as everyone else, price decides.

To be fair, price still matters. Buyers rank pricing information as the most helpful thing on review sites. Price is the ticket to the game. It is not how you win it.

If the cheapest offer mostly won"In our room price did win. With ten minutes and no time to check anything else, price is the easy signal. That is the lesson: if you cannot show a difference, you compete on price."
16A real one: Celonis and Siemens2:00 · 4 clicks

A real one. Celonis, a German software start-up, 2011. Three founders and no connections.

They wrote to chief executives. About 1,500 letters to large companies.

Siemens' audit department tried the software that same year. One department.

From there it spread to logistics, finance, procurement and sales: 6,000 users.

A personal approach got the meeting. A small trial got them in. Results did the rest.

18The Monday-morning list2:00 · 5 clicks

Here is ours. Five moves.

One: map who you already know inside, and who can introduce you. A warm path beats a better pitch.

Two: find the buyer's hidden priority and their boss's rule before you quote.

Three: bring proof the buyer can show upward.

Four: offer a small first step the buyer can defend if it fails.

Five: do not lead with a discount. You are competing with "do nothing", not with a price.

So, how does a start-up sell to a giant? The door is closed because of risk, not because of the product. You open it by lowering the buyer's risk: borrowed trust, proof, a small first step, or a partner.

The pointSell the buyer a smaller risk, not a better product.

Questions?

Questions you may get
  • "Was the game rigged toward connections?" No. A connection gave no points, only information and a place at the top of the inbox.
  • "Is one room enough to prove anything?" No. It is an illustration. That is why we put the research beside it.
  • "How did you decide which start-ups failed?" Each start-up had a hidden reliability on its card. Start-ups without proof were less reliable on average, with a few exceptions.
