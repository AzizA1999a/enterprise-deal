Slides 1 to 6 · The hook: the game

1Title0:45

You are MBA students. Some of you will start your own company. More of you will join one. Either way, one day your product has to win a customer far bigger than you are.

That is a marketing problem before it is a sales problem: which customer you target, what you promise them, and how you earn their trust before you have a track record.

So our question today: how does a start-up sell to a giant? We could explain why that is hard. Instead, you are going to feel it.

The pointThis is the room's own future, and it is marketing: targeting, positioning and trust, aimed at a business buyer.
2You are going to live it0:45

In a minute you will scan a code. Every one of you runs a start-up, and you pick one large company to go after.

Four people at that company have to say yes, one after the other: a manager, security, procurement, and an executive.

Each round you get a small marketing budget: two coins. You spend them on moves. A cold email. A case study. Proof. A pilot. A partner. Your phone shows the chance each one has of working.

One more thing. Each of you knows one person inside one of these companies, and you can introduce two classmates to that person. So the introduction you need is somewhere in this room. Stand up and find it.

Four rounds. Each company signs only its first two start-ups.

3The live game11:30

Joining (2 min). Scan the code now. Type your first name. That is all.

Briefing (1 min). Read your card. Choose the company you go after, and what you lead with: it saves money, it lowers risk, or it gets them there faster. Each company cares most about one of these, and you do not know which. Look at who you know: it may decide your target.

Round 1 (1:40). Go. Two coins. You are at the manager's door. Try something.

Reveal 1 (30 sec). [read: cold outreach, then the move at the top] Look at the cold email. Now open your Network tab. Who in this room knows the person you need?

Round 2 (1:40). On your feet. Find the classmate who knows your next door, and give them your two-digit code.

Reveal 2 (30 sec). [read: how many are past security] Each door wants something different. Read the chances on your phone before you spend.

Round 3 (1:40). Each company signs only two. Some of you are close.

Reveal 3 (30 sec). [read: who has signed, from the buildings] Last round. If you still have an introduction to give, give it: you score when that start-up signs.

Round 4 (1:40). Last coins.

At zero. Sit down. Phones away. Look at the screen.

Slides 7 to 9 · 1 · What is it, and why now?

7What it is, and how big1:00

What you just played has a name. A young company with no track record wins a large organisation as a customer.

Researchers call it using start-ups as suppliers. The large firm gets new technology by buying it, not by investing in the start-up. The start-up brings agility. The corporation brings resources.

How big is the prize? Gartner expects the world to spend 1.47 trillion dollars on enterprise software in 2026, up more than 15 percent in a year.

And among the leading corporations Mind the Bridge surveyed, 90 percent already buy from start-ups.

The pointA huge market, and large companies are already buying from start-ups.
8Why now0:30 · 1 click

Why now? Two shifts.

Of everything enterprises spent on generative-AI applications in 2025, 63 percent went to start-ups. A year earlier it was 36.

67 percent of B2B buyers now say they prefer to buy without a sales rep.

So: more new software than ever, bought by people who would rather not meet a salesperson.

9Three that got in1:15 · 3 clicks

Three real ones.

Harvey, a legal AI start-up. Before Allen and Overy announced a deal, about 3,500 of its lawyers tried a beta and asked 40,000 questions. A small first step.

Celonis, 2011. Three founders wrote about 1,500 letters by hand to large companies. Siemens' audit department tried the software that year. One department.

Snowflake. One early enterprise customer was 17 percent of its revenue. Then existing customers kept spending more: 158 percent of the year before.

Each got in a different way: a small first step, one department, and growth after the first sale. We come back to all three. Over to for how it works.

Slides 10 to 15 · 2 · How and why does it work? (the buyer)

10The whole argument on one slide1:15 · 6 clicks

This is the heart of it. A chain of five links.

One: the supplier is new and unknown. Sociology has a name for it: the liability of newness.

Two: so the buyer feels personal risk.

Three: and it is not one buyer. A committee has to agree.

Four: with that much risk and that many people, doing nothing feels safest.

Five: the deal stalls.

To sell to a giant, you break the chain. You lower the buyer's risk. There are four ways, and we will test each against what you just did.

The pointThe start-up's real task is lowering the buyer's risk.
11The buyer is buying a risk1:15 · 2 clicks

Think about the person behind each door. When your cold email arrived, what were you asking them to do? (take one answer)

To take a risk on a stranger. The buyer is not buying a product. The buyer is buying a risk. If the start-up fails, the buyer answers for it. If the old supplier fails, nobody blames the buyer.

A study of three thousand B2B buyers found that personal value has twice the impact of business value. One fear they named: losing their job over a failed purchase.

On the right, our room: [read: how many cold emails opened a door] That is a stranger asking someone to carry that risk.

12A real start-up faces a committee0:30

In the game you needed four people to say yes. That was the short version.

Gartner says a real buying group has five to sixteen people, across as many as four functions.

Marketing has called this the buying center since 1972: buying is a group decision.

13The safest choice is the one you already made1:45

So: that much risk, and that many people who can say no. What does a careful company do? Nothing. Look at our room again: [read: % past the manager, then % who got the signature]

Every start-up that did not get the signature left its company exactly where it was: with the supplier it already had.

Someone who stood at the same door for two rounds: what was that like? (take one answer)

Harvard Business Review reports that 40 to 60 percent of deals are lost to nobody. The customer says they want to buy, then does nothing.

Forrester finds that 86 percent of B2B purchases stall at some point.

It is old psychology: people stick with the option they already have. Status quo bias.

So a start-up's main competitor is not another start-up. It is "do nothing".

If most of the room got the signature"Our room got further than most real start-ups do. Remember, nobody behind these doors had a job to lose."

Slides 16 to 21 · 2 · How and why does it work? (four ways in)

16Four ways to lower the buyer's risk0:45 · 4 clicks

Four ways to break the chain.

One, borrow trust: arrive through someone the buyer already trusts.

Two, show proof: customers, certifications, results others can check.

Three, shrink the first step: a pilot turns one big yes into a small, safe one.

Four, walk in with a partner who is already inside.

171 · Borrow trust1:30

Everyone in this room knew one person inside one company. Hands up if you used an introduction. And if you gave one?

Here is what it did: [read: share that signed: used an introduction, then never did]

Nobody gave those start-ups a better product. A classmate lent them a name.

In the real world, Bain found that about 90 percent of B2B buyers end up buying from a vendor already on their list on day one.

And when you know nobody? Remember Celonis: no introduction, so about 1,500 handwritten letters.

One honest note. You will see claims like "84 percent of deals start with a referral". We traced it. It leads back to a vendor's marketing page. We found no solid public study comparing warm introductions with cold ones. Our game cannot settle it either: we chose its chances ourselves.

If introductions made little difference"Few of us used them. Finding the right person in a room in four short rounds is hard. It is hard in real life too, which is why founders spend so long on it."
182 · Show proof1:15

Second way: proof. On the left, the security door. [read: show proof, then cold outreach]

Security does not care how good your pitch is. It wants something it can check.

G2 found that 80 percent of organisations require a security or privacy assessment before they buy software.

And here is why buyers insist. Walgreens put Theranos blood tests in its stores. It later closed 40 sites, sued for 140 million dollars, and paid 44 million to patients.

Economists call proof a signal: a costly action that shows what the seller knows. It is not really for the buyer. It is what the buyer shows their boss.

Someone who got stuck at security: what were you missing? (take one answer)

If proof did not stand out at security"With this few tries, luck moves these bars. In a real purchase, legal and security check for weeks."
193 · Shrink the first step1:15

Third way: make the first step small. On the left, the executive's door. [read: offer a small pilot, then the other moves]

The executive is asked to sign for the whole company. A pilot turns that into a small yes.

Notice what it cost you: two coins, your whole budget for a round. A pilot is not free for the start-up either.

A warning from the real world. In a survey of 100 corporate innovation programmes, 81 percent said fewer than a quarter of their start-up pilots became commercial deals. A pilot gets you in the door, not through it.

Palantir told its investors it wins large accounts with pilots at no or low cost, and runs them at a loss.

Remember Harvey: about 3,500 lawyers tried a beta before any deal was announced. That was the small first step.

Someone who offered a pilot: did it work? (take one answer)

204 · Walk in with a partner0:30

Fourth way. On the left, the procurement door. [read: go in with a partner] Procurement asks one question: are you already an approved supplier? A partner is.

McKinsey found start-ups that partnered with one business unit were satisfied 84 percent of the time, against 57 percent for company-wide programmes.

21And price? It was not the way in1:15

What about price? Notice something about our game: it had no prices at all. Nobody could offer a discount.

What you could choose was your message. [read: share that signed: right message, then wrong message]

Bain asked buyers what matters most. They said cost. Then Bain measured what actually drives their loyalty. Cost reduction came 27th out of 36.

Only 14 percent of buyers see enough difference between suppliers to pay more. When you look the same as everyone else, price decides.

Someone who led with "it saves you money": did your company care? (take one answer)

Price is the ticket to the game. It is not how you win it.

If the wrong message did as well as the right one"In our game the message moved the chances only a little, at two of the four doors. An introduction moved them far more."

Slides 22 to 23 · 3 · Who is it best for?

22Should your company sell to giants? Four tests1:45 · 4 clicks

So should every start-up chase giants? No. Four tests.

One: is one deal worth months of selling? An investor's rule of thumb says about ten thousand dollars a year per account rarely pays for a sales team. And a Harvard Business Review classic says: learn how to sell before you hire a sales force.

Two: how big a leap is it for the buyer? Buying from a start-up is what marketing calls a "new task" purchase, the most scrutinised kind. A contained problem that one department owns is far easier than replacing a core system.

Three: can you prove it works? A reference, a certificate, a number the buyer can measure.

Four: does it grow after the first sale? Larger accounts keep and grow their spending far better than small ones.

The pointBig, recurring deals for a contained problem you can prove: go. Cheap, one-off or unprovable: think twice.

Slides 24 to 26 · 4 · Where does it fall short?

24The giant can also hurt you1:15

Now the other side. This can go badly.

BCG found that 45 percent of corporates and 55 percent of start-ups are dissatisfied with their partnerships.

There is an entry cost. A first security audit runs from ten to eighty thousand dollars, often before the first sale.

And depending on one large customer hurts profitability early in the relationship. Remember Snowflake: one customer was 17 percent of its revenue.

So who should stay away, for now? If your price is near ten thousand dollars a year, if you cannot fund a long sale, or if one buyer would be most of your revenue.

The pointSelling to giants is slow, costly to enter, and dangerous if one customer becomes the business.
25When it goes wrong: GT Advanced and Apple1:30 · 4 clicks

Here is one that went wrong. GT Advanced Technologies made sapphire.

October 2013: it signs to supply Apple, exclusively. Apple lends up to 578 million dollars to build the plant. Apple commits to no minimum purchase.

Then Apple decides not to use the sapphire for the iPhone 6 screen. GT Advanced has one customer, and that customer is not buying.

October 2014: bankruptcy, less than a year after signing.

One giant customer can be the whole business, and then the end of it. To be fair: GT Advanced was a listed company, not a start-up, and Apple disputed its version of events. But the structure of the deal is the lesson.

The pointNever accept exclusivity without a commitment to buy.

28If you run that start-up: what to do, in order2:00 · 5 clicks

You just gave us your first moves. Here is the order we would use, so you can compare.

One: pick one kind of buyer and one problem they already feel. Do not chase every famous logo.

Two: find a warm path in. Investors, advisers, former colleagues, their current suppliers.

Three: get your proof ready before the first meeting. The security review will come.

Four: offer a small first step in one business unit, with the next step agreed in writing. No free pilot with no path to a contract.

Five: expand from that unit, and keep selling to others. Remember GT Advanced.

Compare that with your groups. (Name one group whose first move matches a step. Then name a step no group chose, and ask: would you still skip it?)

The pointThe groups' own plans, set against a sequence: target, warm path, proof, small first step, expand.
29Where it is heading1:00 · 1 click

That is what to do today. Where is this going? Three forecasts.

Gartner predicts that by 2028, 90 percent of B2B buying will be intermediated by AI agents: more than 15 trillion dollars of spending.

Yet Gartner also predicts that by 2030, three quarters of buyers will prefer sales experiences that put people ahead of AI.

And Forrester predicts two in three providers will lose deals because buyers cannot verify their claims.

Our reading: software will do the first screening, so proof a machine can check matters more. People will still decide the risky deals, so trust matters as much as ever. Back to to close.

Slides 30 to 31 · To close: what surprised us, and the answer

30What surprised us0:45 · 3 clicks

Looking back over the hour: three things that surprised us. (Say them as your own opinions: "What surprised us was...")

One: the biggest competitor was nobody. 40 to 60 percent of deals are lost to no decision. We expected rival start-ups.

Two: price. Buyers say it matters most, but it ranks 27th of 36 for loyalty. It is the ticket, not the win.

Three: the statistic everyone quotes, 84 percent of deals from referrals, led us to a vendor's marketing page. Check the source before you build a plan on it.

The pointSelling to a giant is less about the product, and less about price, than we expected.
31So, how does a start-up sell to a giant?0:30

We opened with three marketing questions. Here are our answers.

Who you target: one kind of buyer, with one contained problem that a single department owns.

What you promise: a smaller risk, not just a better product.

How you earn trust: borrow it, prove it, and make the first step small.

That is how a start-up sells to a giant.
